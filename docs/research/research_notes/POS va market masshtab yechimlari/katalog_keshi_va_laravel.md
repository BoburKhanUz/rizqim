## Q1. What is the smallest sound design for a cached store directory and menu read model on Redis + Postgres, and what do larger companies' designs show about pitfalls (stale prices, stale stop-lists, stampedes)?

### Takeaway
Large platforms put a read tier in front of the transactional store: a precomputed geo/serviceability index held in memory (Yandex Eda), cached derived data with bounded age and background refresh (Instacart, DoorDash Entity Cache), with stampede control (single-flight, probabilistic early refresh, stale-while-error) as a first-class feature. A few-engineer version is: Postgres stays the source of truth, a Redis-held per-cell store directory plus a versioned per-store menu document, rebuilt by events and by a short TTL, using `Cache::flexible` and atomic locks for stampede protection. No primary source found that documents a delivery company's exact menu-document layout; the layout below is inference.

### Cited Findings
- DoorDash Entity Cache (InfoQ, 20 Jul 2026): transparent HTTP/gRPC caching proxy in the service mesh; over 1.5 million requests per second across 100+ endpoints in 50+ services; hit rate above 90%; upstream requests down 60-95% in normal operation; Kafka event-driven invalidation compares update timestamps with cached responses instead of distributed deletes; dual TTL thresholds let slightly stale data be served during outages (it kept serving "stale but valid" data through a multi-hour upstream outage); lock-free single-flight collapses duplicate work on misses; probabilistic early refresh based on XFetch refreshes hot entries before expiry; about 2.1 ms P99 proxy overhead. Needs a service mesh and platform team. (secondary news write-up of the DoorDash careers-blog post; careers post not read) — [InfoQ, Jul 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/07/doordash-entity-cache-proxy)
- DoorDash standardized on a shared caching library; most common caches are Caffeine (local) and Redis Lettuce (distributed) (search summary, page not read, 2023) — [InfoQ 2023](https://www.infoq.com/news/2023/10/doordash-multilayered-cache/)
- DoorDash inventory platform (search summary, page not read, ~2024): serving layer stores menus as a tree for menu-level reads, while the ingestion layer stores items individually because of frequent item-level writes; it ingests for tens of thousands of convenience/grocery stores with tens of thousands of items each. Different shapes for write and read paths is the key point. — [DoorDash blog](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/how-doordash-designed-a-successful-write-heavy-scalable-and-reliable-inventory-platform/)
- DoorDash partner docs: partners should push menu updates; Auto Menu Pull pulls if no push in the last 24 hours; a processing-complete webhook is sent after ingestion. (Official partner docs, current) — [DoorDash developer docs: ingest and update menus](https://developer.doordash.com/en-US/docs/marketplace/how_to/ingest_and_update_menus/); [Menu Pull](https://developer.doordash.com/en-US/docs/marketplace/how_to/menu_pull/)
- DoorDash homepage (2020, search summary, page not read): homepage rebuilt as a separate "feed service" assembling generic Display Modules, because clients stitched several backend responses and the monolith serving the homepage hit capacity limits; later ranking moved to the Feed service while Search became a pure recall dependency. — [DoorDash ?p=5156](https://careersatdoordash.com/?p=5156)
- Yandex Eda geo index (Habr, 9 Sep 2025, read): about 500,000 restaurants and 3 million delivery zones (about 6 per restaurant); R-tree chosen (geohash rejected for complex zone geometry; H3 rejected because its hierarchy has "holes" that break incremental rebuilds when zones change); index is 10 GB and held entirely in RAM; separate indexes for restaurants and retail, and for pickup (about 60,000 items); geo search about 45 ms p99 / 35 ms p95 (target about 50 ms p99); central Moscow (about 10,000 candidate restaurants) 30-40 ms, smaller cities (about 2,000) 5-10 ms; target index rebuild under 1 minute (heaviest tree builds in 40 s); zone changes should reach results within minutes. — [Habr 945004](https://habr.com/ru/articles/945004)
- Yandex Eda stack (vacancy text, search summary): platform team owns availability of restaurant/menu/dish search and listing services, stack C++17. — [Habr Career vacancy](https://career.habr.com/vacancies/1000128133)
- Instacart (Sep 2023, read): availability scores kept in a database and joined with SQL in retrieval (RPC judged too slow for bulk lookups); full sync from Snowflake several times a day plus "lazy refresh" (an item shown in search whose score is older than the allowed age gets refreshed by background jobs, aggregated through Kinesis); lazy refresh cut ingestion load by about two-thirds; scores must be identical across surfaces; allowed age not stated; no separate cache layer described; 80,000+ retail locations. — [Instacart tech blog](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/instacarts-item-availability-architecture-solving-for-scale-and-consistency)
- Instacart (Feb 2024, InfoQ): three score components with different update rates: general weekly, trending daily/hourly, real-time streamed (Kafka/Flink). — [InfoQ 2024](https://www.infoq.com/news/2024/02/instacart-item-availability/)
- Swiggy serviceability (search summary, page not read, year not confirmed): serviceability is critical because the pre-order flow stops if it fails; distance lookups cached with a geohash key at level 7 for low-traffic origin-destination pairs and level 8 for high-order-volume pairs; cache entries expire on a schedule (partly for recalculation, partly for third-party caching limits); a fallback path exists when the distance service fails; listing page does a point-in-polygon serviceability check and a second check on opening menu/cart. — [Swiggy Bytes part 1](https://bytes.swiggy.com/designing-the-serviceability-platform-at-swiggy-for-high-scale-part-1-751a631f0379); [What serviceability means](https://bytes.swiggy.com/what-serviceability-means-at-swiggy-c94c1aad352a)
- Laravel `Cache::flexible($key, [fresh, stale], $cb)` (docs, Laravel 12 page, 2026): within the fresh window the cache is returned; in the stale window the stale value is returned and a deferred function refreshes it after the response; after the stale window the value is recomputed inline. `Cache::lock` / `Cache::withoutOverlapping` / `Cache::funnel` give atomic locks and concurrency limiting on Redis; cache tags are unsupported on file/dynamodb/database drivers; `Cache::memo()` avoids repeat cache hits within one request. — [Laravel 12 Cache docs](https://laravel.com/docs/12.x/cache)
- spatie/laravel-responsecache v8: default lifetime one week; caches only successful GET text responses; `CacheResponse::for()` fixed lifetime middleware; `FlexibleCacheResponse` with lifetime + grace (stale served while refreshed in background). Per-user/tags/flush details not verified on the README page. — [GitHub README](https://github.com/spatie/laravel-responsecache)

### Inferences
- Smallest sound design (inference from the above, not a documented design of any one company): (1) Postgres tables stay authoritative. (2) A `StoreDirectory` snapshot per geo cell (H3 res 7-8 or geohash 6-7, mirroring Swiggy's level 7/8 split) in Redis holding only ids + coordinates + static card fields, rebuilt by a scheduled job every 30-60 s and on store create/deactivate/zone change events; distance computed in PHP only for stores in the user's cell plus neighbour cells. (3) One versioned menu document per store (`menu:{store_id}:v{n}` + pointer key), built from the normalized tables on menu-change events, TTL as safety net. (4) Serve through `Cache::flexible` with a lock on rebuild so a miss does not stampede Postgres (the same goal as DoorDash single-flight/XFetch, at one-node scale). (5) Catalogue endpoint paginated per store rather than one 129 KB response (Uber Eats pagination from the prior notes supports small first pages).
- The Yandex 500,000-store / 10 GB / in-RAM index shows the extreme end; RIZQIM's store count (not given) is likely orders of magnitude smaller, so a plain Redis snapshot or even an in-process array is sufficient. Platform-team-only: Entity Cache proxy, Kafka timestamp invalidation, Snowflake-to-DB sync, R-tree service. Small-team: cell snapshot, versioned documents, `Cache::flexible` + lock, event-driven bust.
- Pitfall evidence: DoorDash and Instacart both treat bounded staleness as acceptable and make stampede/refresh load a designed property, not an afterthought; Instacart's explicit warning is inconsistency across surfaces (one screen says available, another not), which argues for one availability source feeding every endpoint.

- No primary source for Uber Eats menu/catalogue service, Deliveroo, JET, Glovo, Delivery Hero/Talabat, Grab/GrabFood, Gojek/GoFood, Zomato, iFood, Rappi menu read models. ## Q2. Which data is cached long, which is overlaid in real time, and how are price changes made safe at order time?

### Takeaway
Documented big-platform designs separate slow-changing catalogue content from fast-changing availability and refresh them on different clocks (Instacart weekly/daily/real-time components; DoorDash separate item-level ingestion vs tree serving). No primary source gives an "open now" or stop-list TTL in seconds or an explicit checkout re-validation rule; those pieces are community patterns and inference.

### Cited Findings
- Instacart separates general (weekly), trending (daily/hourly) and real-time (streamed) availability components, and refreshes frequently viewed items more often; full syncs run several times a day to cover the long tail. — [InfoQ 2024](https://www.infoq.com/news/2024/02/instacart-item-availability/); [Instacart 2023](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/instacarts-item-availability-architecture-solving-for-scale-and-consistency)
- DoorDash Entity Cache compares event update timestamps against cached entries (event-driven freshness) with dual TTLs (fresh vs serve-stale-on-failure). — [InfoQ, Jul 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/07/doordash-entity-cache-proxy)
- DoorDash partner API pushes menu changes, with a pull fallback after 24 h without a push: menus are long-lived documents updated by events, with a time-based safety net. — [DoorDash docs](https://developer.doordash.com/en-US/docs/marketplace/how_to/menu_pull/)
- Swiggy (search summary): menu display and serviceability are separate checks (menu may load for a far address but returns non-serviceable instead of an ETA, per a scraper write-up) and serviceability is re-checked when opening the menu/cart. — [Swiggy Bytes](https://bytes.swiggy.com/what-serviceability-means-at-swiggy-c94c1aad352a?gi=bc83b8a48d1e); [DEV scraper write-up (community)](https://dev.to/burahan/swiggy-scraper-in-python-dish-prices-and-menus-by-location-3640)
- Community pattern (open-source PR, not an industry source): public menu cache per restaurant with write invalidation; order creation still re-reads price, availability and a paused flag from the database "so a stale menu can never charge a wrong price"; restaurant status as a short-TTL key plus explicit update. — [Burger-Page PR #112 (community)](https://github.com/heratok/Burger-Page/pull/112); [codemirroring design write-up (community)](https://www.codemirroring.com/system-design/design-food-delivery-tracking/)
- Talabat: partner API has dedicated endpoints for vendor availability, item availability, and an unavailable-items feed, separate from the menu/catalog sync (and claims fewer undelivered/out-of-stock items; marketing copy). — [Talabat docs](https://integration.talabat.com/en/documentation/); [Talabat Developer Portal](https://developer.talabat.com/)
- Meituan (2018): their degradation design lists the core chain as location, merchant recall, goods display and ordering; non-core dependencies are degraded via switches synced in minutes; backend uses multi-level caches and isolation for degradation. — [Meituan Tech 2018](https://tech.meituan.com/2018/05/31/waimai-client-high-availability.html)

### Inferences
- Suggested split (inference, staleness numbers are proposals, not documented): Long (minutes to hours, version-busted on edit): product names, descriptions, photos, categories, modifier structure, base prices in the menu document. Short overlay (5-15 s TTL or push-updated): store open/closed (schedule plus manual pause), stop-list item ids, busy/ETA flags, as one small per-store availability key or a Redis set merged on read. Never cached for correctness: stock decrement, price used for the order total.
- Checkout rule (inference, consistent with the community pattern above): the order endpoint reads authoritative price/availability/open state from Postgres inside the order transaction, compares with the price version/hash the client displayed, and returns a "price/availability changed" response rather than silently charging a different amount. The cached menu is a display copy only.
- Event-driven bust for the small team: POS/merchant edit writes DB, then increments a `menu_version` on the store and dispatches a job that rebuilds the document; stop-list toggles update only the overlay key, so the 129 KB document is not rebuilt for a stop-list change.

### Gaps
- No documented TTL or accepted staleness in seconds for open/closed or stop-lists from DoorDash, Uber, Swiggy, Zomato, Grab, Glovo or others. Instacart gives "allowed age" without a number.
- No primary description of price re-validation at checkout by a delivery company.

## Q3. Geo discovery design (cells, PostGIS, Redis GEO, pagination) and measurements

### Takeaway
Yandex Eda chose R-trees held in RAM over geohash/H3 because delivery zones are polygons that change; Swiggy used geohash levels 7/8 as cache keys for distance lookups. For a store count in the thousands, PostGIS KNN with a GiST index or Redis GEOSEARCH are documented, adequate primitives; the main saving is to stop computing distance over all active stores on every request.

### Cited Findings
- H3 (Uber, 2018): 16 resolutions, 122 base cells, hexagons chosen to minimize quantization error as users move; open-source C library with bindings. The post is about marketplace analytics, not Eats store availability. — [Uber: H3](https://www.uber.com/blog/h3/)
- Yandex Eda explicitly rejected H3 for zone indexing (hierarchy holes break incremental rebuilds) and geohash (complex zone geometry); chose R-tree. — [Habr 945004, Sep 2025](https://habr.com/ru/articles/945004)
- Swiggy: geohash level 7 for low-traffic origin-destination pairs, level 8 for high-volume pairs, as distance cache keys (search summary). — [Swiggy Bytes](https://bytes.swiggy.com/designing-the-serviceability-platform-at-swiggy-for-high-scale-part-1-751a631f0379)
- PostGIS: `<->` in `ORDER BY ... LIMIT` uses a 2D GiST index for true KNN on PostgreSQL 9.5+; index is used only in ORDER BY; one argument must be a constant literal; geography KNN is sphere-based, not spheroid. — [PostGIS docs: `<->`](https://postgis.net/docs/geometry_distance_knn.html)
- Redis `GEOSEARCH` (Redis 6.2+; replaces deprecated GEORADIUS): geo index is a sorted set filled by GEOADD; `BYRADIUS`/`BYBOX`, `ASC`, `COUNT n [ANY]`; complexity O(N+log(M)), N = elements in the grid-aligned bounding box; without `ANY`, work is proportional to matches in the area and a large area with a small COUNT can still be slow. — [Redis GEOSEARCH](https://redis.io/docs/latest/commands/geosearch/)
- Uber Eats pagination (prior notes) and PracHub-style interview material suggest geohash/grid cells with offline-computed hot-store lists in a KV store (interview prep, generic, not DoorDash production) — [PracHub (community)](https://prachub.com/interview-questions/design-homepage-store-recommendations)

### Inferences
- Because delivery zones in Uzbekistan marketplaces are likely radius or polygon per store, the cheap path is: cell key from the user's location, candidate store ids per cell from a rebuilt snapshot (Redis hash/JSON), exact zone test only on candidates. Keep Postgres/PostGIS as the rebuild source with a GiST index and KNN for "nearest N" fallbacks.
- Redis GEO is simplest for "stores within R km sorted by distance" when delivery zone is a radius; it cannot express polygons, so a polygon-zone check must follow on the candidates.
- Pagination: cursor on (distance bucket, store id) from the snapshot, not OFFSET on a recomputed query (inference).

### Gaps
- No published PostGIS vs Elasticsearch vs Redis GEO benchmark for store-sized datasets retrieved. No Elasticsearch/OpenSearch geo_distance doc was fetched. No primary Uber Eats H3 serving document found (H3 use for Eats is only in secondary summaries cited in the earlier notes).

## Q4. Measured effect of FPM pool split, Octane, replicas, caches in Laravel

### Takeaway
There is no controlled published measurement of FPM pool isolation for Laravel; Octane results are workload dependent (about 1.5x-2.5x on DB-backed throughput benchmarks, roughly nothing on I/O-bound services). Read replicas have documented semantics (`sticky`) but no measurements found. For RIZQIM, cutting the per-request work with a cache is likely worth more than a runtime swap (inference).

### Cited Findings
- FPM pool split: only configuration guides and rules of thumb found; no latency measurements. Guides recommend sizing `pm.max_children` as available RAM divided by worker RSS, monitoring the pool "max children reached" counter, static PM to avoid spawn latency (unmeasured claims). — [dchost guide (community, 2025-26)](https://www.dchost.com/blog/en/?p=3710); [Deploynix FPM tuning (community)](https://deploynix.io/blog/tuning-php-fpm-for-laravel-workers-memory-and-process-management); Tideways pool-split example already in prior notes (config only, no numbers) — [Tideways 2016](https://tideways.com/profiler/blog/seperate-your-slow-backend-from-the-important-frontend-traffic-with-php-fpm)
- nginx `limit_req` (official): leaky bucket; `burst=N` queues excess requests up to N then rejects; `nodelay` serves burst immediately but still caps; `delay=D` (1.15.7+) mixes both; `limit_req_status` defaults to 503 (can be set to 429); `limit_req_dry_run` (1.17.1+) counts without limiting; each state is 128 bytes on 64-bit, about 8,000 states per MB, LRU eviction; multiple `limit_req` directives must all pass; they inherit only if the current level defines none. Zones are keyed per variable (per IP, per server), so per-route-group limits need separate `location` blocks with their own zone. — [nginx limit_req docs](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_limit_req_module.html). (`limit_conn` was not covered by the fetched page; its semantics here come from general knowledge, not a source I read.)
- Octane measurements (community, directional; different hardware/tools): Swoole about 1,170 req/s vs FPM about 760 on a trivial route, and about 1,000 vs 540 with 5 queries (Modus Create); wrk, 100 connections, 3-5 Eloquent queries: FPM 10 workers 850 req/s vs Octane+FrankenPHP 4 workers 2,100 req/s (Deploynix); k6, 500 clients: 436 vs 808 req/s, +85% (a Swoole evidence page); an April 2026 post reports Swoole below FPM when using `ab` (HTTP/1.0 short connections) on a light-I/O route. Summarized from a search result; the primary pages were not individually opened except Ttias. — [Modus Create](https://moduscreate.com/?p=20074); [Deploynix](https://deploynix.io/blog/php-fpm-vs-laravel-octane-on-deploynix-real-world-performance-comparison); [Swoole evidence](https://true-async.github.io/en/docs/evidence/swoole-evidence.html); [DEV 2026 comparison](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/laravel-12-octane-frankenphp-vs-swoole-vs-roadrunner-in-2026-1ilf)
- Counter-case (read, community, Oh Dear / Mattias Geniar): moved Octane+FrankenPHP back to FPM; trivial route about 4 ms faster; request times did not improve on busy boxes; peak CPU dropped (frankfurt 49% to 28%, paris 33% to 26%) but memory rose to about 600-670 MB; classic-mode FrankenPHP within about 1% of FPM; custom systemd unit and manually patched binary were operational costs; conclusion: Octane suits CPU-bound framework-heavy work, I/O-bound services gain little. — [ma.ttias.be](https://ma.ttias.be/laravel-octane-vs-php-fpm-lessons-learned/)
- Octane pitfalls (official docs, Laravel 12 page, 2026): app booted once and kept in memory, so singletons holding container/request/config go stale; static arrays leak memory; workers restart after 500 requests by default (`--max-requests`); default one worker per CPU core; 30 s max execution time default; Swoole-only: `Octane::concurrently` (max 1,024 tasks), ticks, Octane cache (up to 2 million ops/s, flushed on restart) and Swoole tables. — [Laravel Octane docs](https://laravel.com/docs/12.x/octane)
- Read/write split (official, Laravel 12 database docs): `read`/`write` host arrays; with several read hosts one is picked randomly per request; `sticky => true` makes reads after a write in the same request cycle use the write connection. Sticky applies only within one request cycle, not across requests. No lag measurements in the docs. — [Laravel database docs](https://laravel.com/docs/12.x/database)

### Inferences
- Pool split protects against worker exhaustion only. CPU and Postgres connections stay shared unless cgroups/containers and a separate read path (cache or replica) are used. Verify with your own load test: run the 250-customer burst with a market pool capped (for example `pm.max_children` sized to RAM) and watch till latency and the FPM status "max children reached" counter (guidance above is unmeasured).
- Given Ttias' result, Octane would mostly help if the 0.16 s -> 9 s degradation is dominated by framework boot and CPU, but not if it is Postgres time on the distance and catalogue queries; a Redis cache that removes those queries is likely a larger and cheaper win (inference).
- Replica `sticky` does not protect a customer who writes an order in one request and reads status in the next from a lagging replica; read-your-write across requests must be handled separately (inference).

### Gaps
- No controlled FPM-pool-isolation measurement; no published replica lag/gain numbers for Laravel; response-cache hit-rate/latency numbers for spatie/laravel-responsecache not found. Octane numbers are vendor/blog level, not reproducible.

## Q5. Marketing-push incidents and the documented way to keep a broadcast from causing a spike

### Takeaway
No public postmortem of a food-delivery push campaign overload was found. Documented practice is vendor guidance: throttle the send rate, host push images on a CDN, queue analytics/webhooks. Telegram's own FAQ caps free bulk sending at about 30 messages per second and says to spread large broadcasts over long intervals.

### Cited Findings
- Telegram Bots FAQ (read): in a single chat avoid more than one message per second; in groups no more than 20 messages per minute; free bulk broadcasts are limited to about 30 messages per second (429 errors beyond that); paid broadcasts enabled via @BotFather raise the ceiling to up to 1,000 messages per second, with messages beyond the free 30/s costing 0.1 Telegram Stars each, only successfully broadcast messages charged; stated requirements: at least 100,000 Stars balance and at least 100,000 monthly active users; the page suggests spreading broadcasts over longer intervals (such as 8-12 hours). — [Telegram Bots FAQ](https://core.telegram.org/bots/faq); contradicted on the threshold by a secondary source that says 10,000 Stars minimum — [Sleekflow (secondary)](https://sleekflow.io/blog/telegram-api). Prefer the official FAQ; the python-telegram-bot wiki notes its rate limiter does not account for `allow_paid_broadcast` — [PTB wiki](https://github.com/python-telegram-bot/python-telegram-bot/wiki/Avoiding-flood-limits)
- Arithmetic from the 30/s limit: 100,000 recipients take about 3,333 s (about 56 min); 10,000 take about 5.6 min (calculation, not sourced).
- OneSignal throttling docs (vendor): per-minute rate divided by 60 and rounded down; setting is global or per message (`throttle_rate_per_minute`); throttled messages must finish within 24 hours; large sends can overload your backend even with low click-through because each open triggers session setup, content fetches, API calls and analytics; throttling spreads opens; images on your own origin cause a download per delivery, fix by CDN; webhook traffic is not rate limited by the vendor, use a queue; web push may re-fetch the Service Worker per subscription, serve from CDN with cache headers. — [OneSignal throttling](https://documentation.onesignal.com/docs/en/throttling)
- General write-up (community): synchronous send-to-all inside an HTTP handler exhausts the thread pool; fix is queues and workers scaled on depth (not an incident report). — [DEV: notification fan-out](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/design-a-notification-fan-out-service-push-email-sms-at-scale-5a0d)
- Shopify edge queue and throttle as the documented "waiting room" analogue, and Meituan's front-end/back-end flow control (backend tags traffic and tells the client, client applies multi-level flow control: show captcha, queue, process, or drop; minute-level degradation config sync; over 20 business functions support degradation; 20 million+ daily orders peak on 19 May 2018). — [Meituan Tech 2018](https://tech.meituan.com/2018/05/31/waimai-client-high-availability.html); Shopify article already in prior notes.
- Zomato NYE/peak, Swiggy IPL: no engineering-level detail retrieved (see prior notes).

### Inferences
- Combined documented practice for RIZQIM (inference): send the Telegram broadcast through a queue at a self-chosen rate well under 30/s (for example 10-20/s), segment audiences into waves, include the web-app deep link rather than heavy images, and make the landing endpoints cache-served (Q1/Q2) so a wave of opens hits Redis, not Postgres. Telegram free tier cannot produce a large instantaneous burst (30/s), so the spike risk comes from the open rate after delivery, which the throttle and a cached catalogue address. Paid broadcast (needs 100,000 MAU per the FAQ) is not usable for a small bot, which in this case limits the self-inflicted spike.
- A virtual waiting room is only needed if the burst still exceeds capacity; a nginx `limit_req` with 503/429 and a cached "busy" page is the small-team version (Shopify pattern).

### Gaps
- No public postmortem of a delivery-app push/broadcast overload. No data on typical open rates after a Telegram broadcast. Virtual waiting room products for food apps: no source found.

## Pattern difficulty summary (inference)

- Large platform team: service-mesh cache proxy with Kafka invalidation (DoorDash), cell architecture, ML availability pipelines (Instacart), in-RAM R-tree geo service (Yandex), cross-region client degradation systems (Meituan).
- Small team can run: cell-keyed Redis directory snapshot, versioned per-store menu document, short-TTL availability overlay, `Cache::flexible` + `Cache::lock`, nginx per-location `limit_req`, separate FPM pool with capped children, throttled queue for Telegram sends, DB re-validation at checkout.
